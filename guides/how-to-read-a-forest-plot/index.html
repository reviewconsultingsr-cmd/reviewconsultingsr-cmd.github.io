How to read a forest plot: a step-by-step guide with an example

Squares, lines, a diamond and an I² value. Here is what each part of a forest plot means, in plain words, using one worked example you can follow line by line.

Dr. Stylianos Sergios Chatziioannou, MD, MBA, 2 MSc · 15 November 2026 · 7 min read

Short answer: a forest plot shows the result of each study as a square with a horizontal line (its confidence interval) and the combined result of the meta-analysis as a diamond. Compare each of them with the vertical line of no effect: if a line or the diamond does not cross it, that result is statistically significant. Then check heterogeneity (I²) to see whether the studies agree.

How to read it in 6 steps
  1. Check the effect measure and the line of no effect
  2. Read each study: square, line, weight
  3. Does the line cross no effect?
  4. Read the diamond
  5. Check heterogeneity
  6. Check which side favours what

1. Check the effect measure and the line of no effect

First look at the label above the numbers. Ratio measures (risk ratio RR, odds ratio OR, hazard ratio HR) have the line of no effect at 1 and are usually drawn on a log scale, so 0.5 and 2 sit at the same distance from 1. Difference measures (mean difference MD, standardised mean difference SMD) have the line of no effect at 0.

2. Read each study: square, line, weight

  • The square is the study's point estimate. In the example, Study A has RR 0.67.
  • The horizontal line is its 95% confidence interval: the range of values compatible with the data (0.34 to 1.31 for Study A).
  • The size of the square shows the study's weight. Larger, more precise studies get more weight. Study D carries 34.9% of the weight, Study C only 5.7%.

A big square does not mean a big effect. It means a precise study.

3. Does the line cross the line of no effect?

If a study's confidence interval crosses 1 (or 0 for differences), that study on its own is not statistically significant. In the example, all six studies cross 1. None of them alone shows a clear benefit.

4. Read the diamond

The diamond at the bottom is the pooled result. Its centre is the combined estimate and its width is the 95% confidence interval. In the example the pooled RR is 0.74 (95% CI 0.60 to 0.91): the diamond sits entirely to the left of 1, so the combined result is statistically significant. In plain words: in this illustrative example the intervention reduces the risk of the event by about 26% (1 minus 0.74).

This is the point of a meta-analysis: six small studies that were each inconclusive, combined, give a precise and significant answer.

5. Check heterogeneity

Below the diamond you will find the heterogeneity statistics. I² tells you what percentage of the variation between studies is real rather than chance. As a rough guide from the Cochrane Handbook: 0 to 40% might not be important, 30 to 60% may be moderate, 50 to 90% may be substantial and 75 to 100% considerable (Deeks et al., 2024). In the example I² is 0%, so the studies agree well. You can also see it by eye: the confidence intervals overlap.

With high heterogeneity, do not stop at the diamond. Look for the reason (different populations, doses, follow-up or risk of bias) with subgroup or sensitivity analyses, and prefer a random-effects model, which accounts for differences between studies.

6. Check which side favours what

Under the axis you usually see labels such as "Favours intervention" and "Favours control". Always read them. Whether left means better depends on the outcome: for deaths or complications, fewer is better (left of 1); for an outcome like remission or pregnancy, more is better (right of 1).

Make your own forest plot in R

With the free meta package in R, a forest plot for binary outcomes takes a few lines:

library(meta)
m <- metabin(event.e, n.e, event.c, n.c, studlab = study,
             data = mydata, sm = "RR",
             common = FALSE, random = TRUE)
forest(m)

In SPSS Statistics 28 and later, the Meta Analysis procedures under the Analyze menu also produce a forest plot.

The 5 most common mistakes

  1. Reading "not significant" as "no effect". A wide interval that crosses 1 usually means too little data.
  2. Thinking a bigger square means a bigger effect. It means more weight.
  3. Forgetting the log scale for ratios.
  4. Ignoring the "favours" labels and reading the direction the wrong way.
  5. Reporting the diamond without mentioning heterogeneity.

Frequently asked questions

What does the diamond in a forest plot mean?

The diamond is the pooled result of the meta-analysis. Its centre is the combined effect estimate and its width is the 95% confidence interval. If the diamond does not touch the line of no effect, the pooled result is statistically significant.

What does it mean if a confidence interval crosses 1?

For ratios such as the risk ratio or odds ratio, a 95% confidence interval that crosses 1 means the result is not statistically significant at the 5% level. It does not prove there is no effect: the study may simply be too small to tell.

Is the line of no effect always at 1?

No. It is at 1 for ratio measures (risk ratio, odds ratio, hazard ratio) and at 0 for difference measures (mean difference, standardised mean difference, risk difference).

What is I² and what is a high value?

I² is the percentage of variation across studies that is due to real differences rather than chance. As a rough guide from the Cochrane Handbook: 0 to 40% might not be important, 30 to 60% may be moderate, 50 to 90% may be substantial and 75 to 100% considerable heterogeneity.

Can I make a forest plot in R or SPSS?

Yes. In R, the meta and metafor packages produce publication-quality forest plots with a few lines of code. SPSS Statistics 28 and later also includes meta-analysis procedures that draw a forest plot.
