How to do a systematic review: a 9-step guide for students

Have you been asked to do a systematic review for your thesis, or do you want to publish one and don't know where to start? This guide walks you through the process in the order it happens in practice, with examples and the mistakes I see most often.

Dr. Stylianos Sergios Chatziioannou, MD, MBA, 2 MSc · 15 November 2026 · 10 min read
The 9 steps
  1. Research question with PICO or PCC
  2. Check the review does not already exist
  3. Protocol and registration
  4. Search strategy
  5. Study selection by two reviewers
  6. Data extraction
  7. Risk of bias
  8. Synthesis and meta-analysis
  9. Writing according to PRISMA 2020

A systematic review is not just a long literature review. It is a study with its own methodology, where every decision (what you search for, where, which studies you keep and why) is defined in advance and recorded, so that someone else can repeat it and find the same results.

1. Frame your question with PICO or PCC

Everything starts with a focused question. For effectiveness questions we use PICO: Population, Intervention, Comparison, Outcome.

Example: In adults with type 2 diabetes (P), does cognitive behavioural therapy (I) compared with usual care (C) reduce depressive symptoms (O)?

If you want to map a field rather than answer an effectiveness question, you probably need a scoping review with PCC: Population, Concept, Context. Your inclusion and exclusion criteria follow directly from PICO.

2. Check that it does not already exist

Before you start, search PROSPERO, the Cochrane Library and PubMed with the "Systematic Review" filter. If a recent review already answers the same question, you can narrow the question, focus on a different population or update it with studies published since. Committees and reviewers almost always ask: "What does your review add?"

3. Write a protocol and register it

The protocol describes in advance the question, criteria, databases, search strategy, how studies will be selected, the quality appraisal tool and the analysis plan. For health-related reviews, registration is free on PROSPERO. For other fields or for scoping reviews, OSF is commonly used. Registration protects you from the criticism that you changed the methods after seeing the results.

4. Build your search strategy

For each PICO element, list keywords and synonyms, and in PubMed also use MeSH terms. Then combine them with operators:

OperatorWhat it doesExample
ORJoins synonyms of the same concept and broadens the searchdepression OR depressive
ANDConnects different concepts and narrows the search(diabetes) AND (cognitive behavioral therapy)
* and " "Covers word endings or searches an exact phrasedepress* · "type 2 diabetes"

Search at least two or three databases, such as PubMed, Scopus or Embase and the Cochrane Library, and record the date and number of results for each search. Include the full strategy for one database in an appendix.

5. Select studies with two reviewers

Import all results into a tool such as Rayyan (free) or Covidence and remove duplicates. Selection happens in two phases: first titles and abstracts, then full texts. Two people decide independently and disagreements are resolved by discussion or a third person. At the full-text stage, record the reason for exclusion for every study, because you will need it for the PRISMA flow diagram.

6. Extract the data

Create a form (usually in Excel) with everything you need from each study: authors and year, country, design, sample, intervention, comparison, outcomes and results. Pilot the form on two or three studies before continuing, because it almost always needs corrections.

7. Assess risk of bias

Each study is appraised with a tool suited to its design:

Study typeCommon tool
Randomised controlled trialsCochrane RoB 2
Non-randomised studies of interventionsROBINS-I
Cohort and case-control studiesNewcastle-Ottawa Scale
Various designs, qualitative studiesJBI critical appraisal checklists

8. Synthesis: meta-analysis or narrative

If studies are similar enough in population, intervention and outcomes, you perform a meta-analysis with a forest plot, heterogeneity assessment (I²) and publication bias tests. If not, you do a narrative synthesis, ideally following the SWiM guideline. In both cases, the certainty of the evidence is often rated with GRADE.

9. Write it up according to PRISMA 2020

PRISMA 2020 is the reporting guideline for systematic reviews. It includes a 27-item checklist and the flow diagram showing how many studies you found, how many you excluded at each stage and why. Most journals require the completed checklist with your submission.

The 5 most common mistakes

  1. A question that is too broad, leading to thousands of studies that cannot be screened.
  2. Searching only one database, or without synonyms.
  3. Study selection by a single person without reporting it.
  4. The wrong quality appraisal tool for the study design.
  5. A meta-analysis of studies that should not have been combined.

Frequently asked questions

How many databases do I need?

At least two, usually three or more. In health sciences the most common are PubMed/MEDLINE, Scopus or Embase and the Cochrane Library; in psychology, PsycINFO is added.

Can I do a systematic review on my own?

Study selection and quality appraisal are normally done by two people independently. In a thesis, the second reviewer can be a fellow student or your supervisor, and this is reported in the methods.

How long does it take?

For a thesis, expect 2 to 6 months. Time depends mainly on the number of search results and whether a meta-analysis is included.
