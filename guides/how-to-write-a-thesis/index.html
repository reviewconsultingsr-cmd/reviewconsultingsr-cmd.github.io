How to write a bachelor's or master's thesis: a step-by-step guide

From choosing a topic to the final defence, in 10 clear steps. With a chapter-by-chapter structure, a sample timeline and the mistakes that cost students the most time.

Dr. Stylianos Sergios Chatziioannou, MD, MBA, 2 MSc · 18 November 2026 · 10 min read

Short answer: a good thesis starts with a narrow, answerable question that your supervisor approves, a method that fits the time you have, and a written plan with deadlines. After that, the work is reading, collecting or analysing data, and writing chapter by chapter, with your supervisor reviewing drafts along the way.

The 10 steps
  1. Choose a feasible topic
  2. Agree the rules with your supervisor
  3. Turn the topic into a research question
  4. Choose the type of thesis
  5. Write the proposal
  6. Ethics and data protection
  7. Search and read the literature
  8. Collect and analyse your data
  9. Write the chapters
  10. Edit, check and prepare the defence

1. Choose a feasible topic

The best topic is not the most impressive one. It is the one you can finish well in the time you have. A useful test is FINER: is the question feasible, interesting, novel, ethical and relevant (Hulley et al., 2013)?

  • Feasible: can you get the data, the participants or the studies within your deadline?
  • Interesting to you: you will live with this topic for months.
  • Close to your supervisor's expertise: you will get better feedback.

Write three candidate topics in one sentence each and take them to your first meeting.

2. Agree the rules with your supervisor

Before you write anything, find your department's thesis regulations and ask your supervisor about:

  • the expected length and structure
  • the referencing style (often APA 7 or Vancouver)
  • how often you will meet and how you will send drafts
  • the deadlines for the proposal, the full draft and the submission
  • the rules on the use of AI tools

Write these down and send a short summary email after the meeting. It saves misunderstandings later.

3. Turn the topic into a research question

A topic ("stress in nurses") is not a question, and the move from one to the other is the core of any research project (Booth et al., 2016). A question tells you exactly what to look for: "What is the prevalence of burnout among intensive care nurses in public hospitals?" Frameworks help you make it precise:

  • PICO (population, intervention, comparison, outcome) for questions about effects
  • PCC (population, concept, context) for scoping questions
  • PEO (population, exposure, outcome) for questions about associations or experiences

Then write one aim and two to four specific objectives. Every later chapter should serve them.

4. Choose the type of thesis

Many departments accept more than one design. Choose the one that fits your question, your time and your access to data.

TypeWhat you doGood for
Literature reviewSearch, select and critically discuss published work on a topic.Bachelor's theses and background chapters
Systematic reviewAnswer a focused question with a protocol, a documented search, two reviewers and PRISMA 2020 reporting (Page et al., 2021).Master's theses that can become a publication
Scoping reviewMap all the evidence on a broad topic and identify gaps.New or broad topics
Quantitative studyCollect numerical data, for example with a questionnaire, and analyse it statistically.Questions about how much, how often or what is associated
Qualitative studyInterviews or focus groups, analysed for themes.Questions about experiences and meanings

A systematic review needs no participants or ethics approval, which makes it a practical choice when time is short. See how to do a systematic review in 9 steps.

5. Write the proposal

Most proposals are 2 to 5 pages and contain the same parts:

  1. A working title
  2. Background: why the question matters, in one or two paragraphs
  3. Aim, objectives and research question
  4. Methods: design, participants or sources, data collection and analysis
  5. Ethical considerations
  6. A timeline
  7. Key references

A clear proposal is the fastest way to get your supervisor's approval, and it becomes the first draft of your methods chapter.

6. Ethics and data protection

If your study involves people (questionnaires, interviews, patient records), you will usually need approval from your institution's ethics committee before you collect any data. Plan for informed consent, anonymised data and secure storage in line with the GDPR. Ask early: approvals can take weeks.

7. Search and read the literature

Search databases relevant to your field (for example PubMed, Scopus or Web of Science), not only Google. Keep a simple search log: the database, the date, the search terms and the number of results. Store every source in a reference manager from day one, so your reference list is built as you go. While you read, note for each study its aim, design, sample, main findings and limitations. Those notes become your literature review.

8. Collect and analyse your data

Follow the methods you described in the proposal. For quantitative data, plan the analysis before you collect the data: which variables, which tests, and why. R and SPSS are both widely accepted. For qualitative data, transcribe the interviews and analyse them systematically, for example with thematic analysis. For reviews, extract data with a pre-tested form and assess the quality of each study.

9. Write the chapters

The exact structure depends on your department, but most theses follow this order. The word shares are a rough guide for an empirical thesis.

ChapterWhat it containsApproximate share
AbstractBackground, aim, methods, results and conclusion in 200 to 300 words.1 page
IntroductionThe problem, why it matters, the gap and your aim.10%
Literature reviewWhat is already known, organised by themes, ending with the gap your thesis fills.25%
MethodsDesign, sample, tools, procedure, analysis and ethics, in enough detail to be repeated.15%
ResultsYour findings with tables and figures, without interpretation.20%
DiscussionWhat the results mean, how they compare with other studies, strengths and limitations.20%
ConclusionThe answer to your question and recommendations for practice and research.5%
References and appendicesEvery source cited in the text, plus questionnaires, consent forms and extra tables.As needed

Write the methods first, while the details are fresh, then the results, the discussion and the literature review. Write the introduction, the conclusion and the abstract last.

10. Edit, check and prepare the defence

  • Read the whole thesis once for logic and once for language.
  • Check that every in-text citation is in the reference list and the other way round, in the required style (American Psychological Association, 2020).
  • Run the similarity check your university uses and follow its rules on AI tools.
  • Prepare a 10 to 15 minute presentation: question, methods, main results, limitations and what your work adds.
  • Expect questions on why you chose your method, what your limitations are and what you would do differently.

A sample timeline

An illustrative plan for a master's thesis of about six months. A bachelor's thesis often follows the same steps in less time.

MonthMain work
1Topic, research question, supervisor agreement
2Proposal and, if needed, ethics application
3Literature search and reading, methods chapter
4Data collection or study selection
5Analysis, results and discussion
6Introduction, abstract, editing and defence

The 6 most common mistakes

  1. A topic that is too broad to answer in one thesis.
  2. Starting to write before the question and method are approved.
  3. Collecting data without a plan for the analysis.
  4. A literature review that summarises studies one by one instead of comparing them.
  5. Leaving the references for the end.
  6. Sending the supervisor one full draft at the last minute instead of chapters along the way.

Frequently asked questions

How long should a bachelor's or master's thesis be?

It depends on your department's regulations, which set the word count or page range. Always check them before you plan your chapters.

Can my master's thesis be a systematic review?

Often yes, if your department accepts it. A systematic review is a recognised research design, needs no participants and can be published afterwards.

Can I use AI tools such as ChatGPT?

Follow your university's policy. Many allow limited use, such as checking language, as long as you disclose it. You remain responsible for every sentence and every reference.

Will you write my thesis for me?

No. We provide methodological guidance, training and feedback so that the work stays yours and meets your university's academic integrity rules.

When should I ask for help?

As early as possible, ideally when you choose the question and method. Fixing a design problem at the start takes hours; fixing it at the end can take months.
