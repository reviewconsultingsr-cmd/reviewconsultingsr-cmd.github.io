What is a meta-analysis?

A meta-analysis is the statistical method that combines the results of several studies into one overall estimate, more precise than any single study. It usually comes at the end of a systematic review, when studies are similar enough in population, intervention and outcomes.

Most errors reviewers point out are not in the software but in the choices: the wrong effect measure, fixed effects despite high heterogeneity, double-counted patients or conclusions the data do not support. That is where I help.

The steps of a meta-analysis

We can do them together from the start, or I can check an analysis you have already run.

Feasibility check

We check whether the studies can be pooled or whether a narrative synthesis is the better choice.

Effect measure

Choosing between OR, RR, HR, mean difference (MD) or standardised mean difference (SMD), depending on your data.

Data extraction and conversion

Collecting numbers from each study and converting them, for example from median and IQR to mean and SD.

Fixed or random effects model

Choosing the model and estimation method (e.g. REML, Hartung-Knapp) with a justification reviewers accept.

Heterogeneity

I², tau², prediction intervals, subgroup analyses and meta-regression where it makes sense.

Publication bias and sensitivity

Funnel plots, Egger's test, leave-one-out analysis and certainty of evidence with GRADE.

Reporting the results

Publication-ready forest plots, tables and results text following PRISMA 2020.

Frequently asked questions

How many studies do I need for a meta-analysis?

Technically two are enough, but with fewer than five to ten studies, estimates of heterogeneity and publication bias tests are unreliable. In the assessment I tell you what is realistic for your data.

Fixed or random effects?

Random effects are usually the safer choice when studies differ in population or design. Fixed effects only make sense when studies are essentially identical.

What does a high I² mean?

That much of the variation between studies is not due to chance. It does not invalidate the meta-analysis, but it needs to be explored with subgroup or sensitivity analyses.
