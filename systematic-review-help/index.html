What is a systematic review?

A systematic review answers a focused research question by identifying and appraising all relevant studies with a predefined, transparent and reproducible method. Unlike a narrative literature review, it follows a protocol, searches several databases with a documented strategy, assesses study quality and is reported according to PRISMA 2020. When the data allow, it includes a meta-analysis.

It is one of the most common designs for master's theses and one of the strongest article types to publish, but also the one students find hardest: the rules are strict, and a mistake at the start shows at the end.

The steps of a systematic review

You can ask for help with every stage or only the ones you find difficult. For a detailed walkthrough with examples, read the guide: how to do a systematic review in 9 steps.

Research question (PICO or PCC)

We turn your idea into a focused, answerable question and set clear inclusion and exclusion criteria.

Protocol and PROSPERO registration

We write the protocol and register it, which most journals now expect.

Search strategy

Keywords, MeSH terms, Boolean operators and searches in PubMed, Scopus, Web of Science, Cochrane and other relevant databases.

Study selection by two reviewers

Title, abstract and full-text screening with tools such as Rayyan or Covidence, recorded in a PRISMA 2020 flow diagram.

Data extraction and risk of bias

An extraction form plus quality appraisal with RoB 2, ROBINS-I, Newcastle-Ottawa or JBI checklists, depending on study design.

Synthesis and meta-analysis

Narrative synthesis or meta-analysis with forest plots, heterogeneity (I²), sensitivity analysis and publication bias tests.

Writing and submission

PRISMA 2020 structure, tables and figures, journal selection and responses to reviewers.

Frequently asked questions

How long does a systematic review take?

Usually 2 to 6 months, depending on the scope of the question, the number of studies and whether a meta-analysis is included. The free assessment gives you a realistic timeline for your topic.

What is the difference between a systematic and a literature review?

A literature review describes a topic freely. A systematic review follows a protocol, searches several databases with a documented strategy, selects studies with two reviewers, appraises their quality and is reported according to PRISMA 2020.

Do I always need a meta-analysis?

No. A meta-analysis is only done when studies are similar enough to be combined statistically. Otherwise we use a narrative synthesis, which is equally acceptable.

Will you write the review for me?

No. I provide methodological guidance, training and technical support so the work remains yours and meets your institution's academic integrity rules. The scope of support is agreed from the start.

Can you help with reviews outside medicine?
